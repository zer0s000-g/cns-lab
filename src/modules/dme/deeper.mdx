### Distance from time

The aircraft starts a stopwatch when it sends its pulse pair and stops it when the answer arrives. The ground station always waits the same reply delay *T*<sub>d</sub> before answering, so

<Formula>{`distance = (t − T_d) × c / 2

X channels: T_d = 50 µs
Y channels: T_d = 56 µs`}</Formula>

Radio needs about **12.36 µs** for one nautical mile there and back. An answer after 421 µs on an X channel means (421 − 50) ÷ 12.36 ≈ **30 NM**.

### Slant range, not ground distance

The DME measures the straight line to the antenna. With ground distance *g* and height *h* above the station (both in NM; 1 NM ≈ 6,076 ft):

<Formula>{`slant range = √(g² + h²)

Right overhead (g = 0): slant range = h
e.g. 6,000 ft → about 1.0 NM`}</Formula>

Far from the station the difference is tiny: at 10,000 ft and 30 NM out, the DME reads 30.04 NM for 30.00 NM over the ground. It only matters close to the station, and most at high altitude.

### Groundspeed and time to station

The avionics compare successive distances and smooth the result. What they get is the rate of change of the slant range:

<Formula>{`DME speed = (g / slant range) × v × |cos α|`}</Formula>

where *v* is the real groundspeed and α the angle between your track and the line to the station. Straight in or out (α = 0° or 180°) far away, DME speed ≈ *v*. Circling the station (α = 90°) it is zero. Close to the station the factor *g* / slant range drops, so the reading falls to almost nothing as you pass overhead. Time to station is distance ÷ closure speed.

### Frequencies and channels

DME uses the **UHF band from 962 to 1213 MHz**: 126 channels in each of two modes, X and Y, 252 in all. The aircraft asks on 1025–1150 MHz; the station answers **63 MHz** above or below.

| Mode | Question spacing | Answer spacing | Reply delay |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 12 µs | 12 µs | 50 µs |
| Y | 36 µs | 30 µs | 56 µs |

Every VOR and ILS frequency is <Term id="dme-pairing">paired</Term> with one DME channel, so the pilot tunes both with one knob:

| VHF frequency | DME channel | Question / answer |
|---|---|---|
| 108.00 MHz (VOR) | 17X | 1041 / 978 MHz |
| 110.30 MHz (ILS localizer) | 40X | 1064 / 1001 MHz |
| 114.30 MHz (the CNS VOR in this simulator) | 90X | 1114 / 1177 MHz |

The DME sends its Morse identification on a **1350 Hz** tone, one ident period in four of its paired VOR.

### Many aircraft, one station

The interrogator asks about **150 times a second** while searching and at most about **30 times a second** once it is tracking (<Term id="dme-search-track">search and track</Term>). Its questions are spaced randomly (**jitter**). Only answers to its own questions keep arriving at the same delay, so it looks for that line-up and ignores the rest.

The ground station is designed for about **100 aircraft** and can send up to about **2,700 answers a second**. After each answer its receiver is blind for about **60 µs** (<Term id="dme-dead-time">dead time</Term>). When few aircraft ask, it fills the gaps with random <Term id="dme-squitter">squitter</Term> pairs so it transmits at least about 700 pairs a second. When too many ask, it lowers its receiver sensitivity: the weakest questions, from the farthest aircraft, stop getting answers first, and their <Term id="dme-reply-efficiency">reply efficiency</Term> drops to zero.

<Note title="Honest simplifications">
The simulator uses a flat 60 µs dead time, treats the questions of all aircraft as random in time, lets traffic interrogate at a fixed rate (1 in 20 aircraft searching) and checks only the radio horizon (not hills) for the other traffic, models the sensitivity reduction as a sharp distance cut-off and uses the 1.23·(√h₁ + √h₂) radio-horizon rule. Measured distances have no noise. Real avionics use more elaborate search and memory logic.
</Note>
