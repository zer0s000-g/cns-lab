### Frequencies and modulation

Aeronautical HF voice uses bands between about **2.85 and 22 MHz**, with **single sideband** (upper sideband, called J3E): only one copy of the voice spectrum is sent and the carrier is suppressed, so all the power goes into a channel about 3 kHz wide. Frequencies are organised in "families" spread over several bands, so that a crew can always find one that suits the time of day and the distance.

### The ionosphere

Sunlight knocks electrons off gas molecules high above the Earth. The free electrons bend HF waves back down.

| Layer | Height | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| D | about 60–90 km | Only by day. Does not reflect HF; it **absorbs**, much more at low frequencies. |
| E | about 100–120 km | Reflects by day (up to about 3–4 MHz straight up); very weak at night. |
| F1 | about 200 km | Only by day. |
| F2 | about 250–400 km | The main HF mirror. At night F1 and F2 merge into one F layer near 300 km. |

The **critical frequency** foF2 is the highest frequency the F2 layer returns when you transmit straight up: in this simulator about 10 MHz in the afternoon and 4 MHz before dawn. Real values run from about 6–12 MHz by day to 3–5 MHz at night and change with the 11-year solar cycle, the season and latitude.

### The secant law and hop distance

A wave leaving the ground at elevation angle β meets a layer at (virtual) height h at an incidence angle i from the vertical. On a round Earth of radius R = 6,371 km:

<Formula>{`sin i = R · cos β / (R + h)

reflected if   f ≤ fc / cos i   ( = fc · sec i )
hop distance   D = 2 · R · (π/2 − β − i)`}</Formula>

A slanting wave (small β, large i) is reflected at a much higher frequency than one going straight up. For a given distance, the angle is fixed, so the path has a **maximum usable frequency**, MUF = fc · sec i. Above it the wave escapes into space. A low ray off the F2 layer covers up to about 3,500–4,000 km in one hop; longer paths need several hops, reflecting off the sea in between.

Operators aim a little below the MUF, at the **optimum working frequency**:

<Formula>{`OWF ≈ 0.85 × MUF`}</Formula>

By day the MUF is high (and low frequencies are absorbed); at night the MUF drops. So HF operators move to a higher band in the morning and a lower one in the evening.

### The skip zone

The ground wave dies out within a few tens to a few hundred kilometres. Above the critical frequency, steep rays escape, so the first sky wave lands some distance away. In between is the **skip zone**: nothing is heard there, although stations farther away hear the signal clearly. Below the critical frequency even a vertical ray comes back, and there is no skip zone.

### Absorption and noise

The D layer absorbs roughly in proportion to 1/f² and to the slant path through it (sec i). The simulator treats a ray that loses more than 30 dB (99.9 % of its power) as absorbed.

<Formula>{`D-layer loss per pass ≈ A × (10 MHz / f)² × sec i      (A: about 1.5 dB at noon, almost 0 at night)`}</Formula>

A **solar flare** sends a burst of X-rays that multiplies D-layer absorption on the sunlit side of the Earth for minutes to hours: an HF blackout (a sudden ionospheric disturbance). The night side is not affected.

HF receivers also pick up a lot of **noise**: man-made and galactic background, and static from thunderstorms, which can travel thousands of kilometres. All of it is stronger at lower frequencies.

### SELCAL

Selective calling saves the crew from listening to HF noise for hours. Each aircraft has a four-letter code. The ground station sends two pulses, each two tones at once for about 1 second, with about 0.2 seconds between them (for example "AB-CD": A + B, then C + D). The decoder in the called aircraft rings a chime and lights a light; everyone else stays quiet.

### Why HF voice is being replaced

HF worked over the oceans for decades, but it is noisy, it depends on the Sun, controllers talk to aircraft through a radio operator who relays each message, and a busy frequency makes position reports slow. Many oceanic regions now use:

- **CPDLC**: standard text messages between controller and pilot, sent over satellite (or VHF near land). No mishearing, and no waiting for a quiet frequency.
- **SATCOM** voice and data: satellites reach almost everywhere and do not care about the ionosphere.
- **ADS-C**: the aircraft reports its position automatically.

HF remains the backup, and <Term id="hf-data-link">HF data link</Term> (HFDL) carries the same kind of data messages over HF where satellites are not available, such as over the poles.

<Note title="Honest simplifications">
The ionosphere is modelled as smooth mirrors at fixed virtual heights, the same all along the path, at a place on the equator at the equinox. Real rays bend gradually inside each layer, the layers tilt and ripple, and the E layer can come and go in patches. Fading is a simple wobble. The direct wave to a high aircraft near the station uses the same line-of-sight rule as VHF.
</Note>
